The 1.5°C Carbon Clock.

The world has a fixed, shrinking budget of CO2 it can still emit and keep a reasonable chance of holding warming to 1.5°C. This clock counts it down in real time, based on the IPCC's own published figures, so the number keeps ticking whether or not anyone is watching.

How this is calculated

Methodology.

The starting budget

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (2021) estimated a remaining global carbon budget of 500 billion tonnes of CO2 from the start of 2020, for a 50% chance of keeping warming below 1.5°C. This is a widely cited, peer-reviewed figure, not an original ClimateZero estimate.

The depletion rate

We use the same global annual CO2 emissions figure published elsewhere on this site: 36.8 GtCO2 in 2023. The clock simply subtracts emissions from the remaining budget at a constant per-second rate based on this figure, it is a simplified linear model, not a scientific projection of future emissions.

What this does and doesn't mean

Reaching zero on this clock does not mean climate change "ends" there, it marks the point past which holding warming to 1.5°C with even a 50% likelihood becomes very difficult without large-scale carbon removal. Warming continues on a spectrum well beyond that point.

Inspiration

This format follows in the tradition of long-running carbon budget clocks such as the MCC Berlin Carbon Clock and Climate Clock, both of which are excellent, more detailed tools worth exploring directly.
